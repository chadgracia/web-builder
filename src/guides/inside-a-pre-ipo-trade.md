# Inside a Pre-IPO Trade

## How private secondaries actually work — and where investors get burned

**By Chad Gracia** — Registered Representative, Rainmaker Securities, LLC (member FINRA/SIPC)

*This guide reflects my personal experience and opinions from ten years brokering private secondary transactions. It is educational material — not investment advice, not an offer to sell or a solicitation of an offer to buy any security, and not a substitute for your own advisors. Private securities are speculative, illiquid, and suitable only for accredited investors who can withstand the loss of their entire investment.*

---

Most of what's written about pre-IPO investing is written by people who have never closed a trade. They can tell you what an SPV is. They can't tell you why a transaction that both sides wanted, at a price both sides agreed to, died in week six — or how to see it coming in week one.

I can. Over the past decade I've brokered nearly $200M in private secondary transactions, including some of the hardest names in the market to actually deliver. I've also watched deals collapse for every reason on the menu: blocked transfers, phantom shares, stacked fees, sellers who never had the right to sell, buyers whose money existed mostly as intention.

This guide is what I'd want in hand before wiring a dollar into this market. Five parts: how a real trade actually runs, start to settlement; the flavors of SPVs and which to avoid; direct shares versus SPVs and how buyers actually get directs; what to know before trading through a platform; and the red flags — with the specific questions that expose them.

## Part 1: The Anatomy of a Real Trade

Let me walk you through an actual transaction — a direct share transfer in a private robotics company, details anonymized, mechanics exactly as they happened. The average secondary transaction has more than twenty distinct steps. Here's how they group.

**Step 1: The indication (day 1).** A buyer tells me what he wants: this company, roughly this size, at or under this price. Not "I'm interested in AI companies" — an actionable indication names a company, a size, and a price. In this case: a family office wanting a low-seven-figure position in a name where I'd closed before.

**Step 2: Engagement and compliance (days 1–7).** Before anything else, U.S. brokers are required to conduct KYC ("Know Your Client") and AML (anti-money-laundering) checks. The seller signs a non-exclusive engagement, and both sides complete onboarding. This step feels bureaucratic, and a counterparty who resists basic compliance can be a red flag — though usually it just means they’re busy, and nobody enjoys entering personal information on the web. There is no way around it, however, and we use encryption to keep your data safe.

**Step 3: Sourcing the seller (days 3–14).** This is where most "deals" die quietly, because most offered supply doesn't exist. Many sellers claim to have access to shares; they very often come up empty-handed. Real supply is a specific person — usually an employee or ex-employee holding common stock, sometimes an early fund rebalancing — with a verified position and the legal right to sell it. In this trade, the seller was a current employee selling a portion of vested shares for household purposes: a house, in fact. That detail matters more than it looks, and I'll come back to it.

**Step 4: The match (days 10–21).** My job is to bring both sides to firm agreement on price, size, and structure *before* they ever meet. Price discovery in an illiquid market is negotiation informed by data: recent crossed trades, firm bids and asks, company ROFR levels, the last primary round. When both sides are firm, I make the introductions by email.

**Step 5: The Share Transfer Notice and the ROFR clock (days 21–51).** I prepare a Transfer Notice, which includes all the relevant details about the trade and send it to the company after buyer and seller sign. This starts the Right of First Refusal period — typically 30 days, sometimes shorter, occasionally a bit longer, depending on the bylaws and the company’s board calendar. During ROFR, the company (or in some cases early investors) can step in and buy the shares at the price in the Transfer Notice. Understand what this means for you as a buyer: **you can negotiate a great price and lose the trade because of it.** If you're buying meaningfully below fair value, you've effectively made the company an attractive offer to keep the shares. The seller still gets paid either way; the buyer walks away empty-handed. The company can also simply block the transfer, if its bylaws allow — and many do. Both the ROFR level and the threat of being blocked are things I will have discussed with my client long before and we will have worked to ensure that neither occurs.

**Step 6: The SPA and settlement (days 45–60).** ROFR expires unexercised, and the parties sign the Share Purchase Agreement. Buyer wires. Company countersigns. The ledger — usually Carta — updates, and the buyer is on the cap table. In this transaction, signing to settled took nine days. Fifty days end to end is a conservative and realistic expectation for a direct transfer. An SPV interest, by contrast, can often close within a week — one of its genuine advantages, which we'll get to.

**Why this trade closed when others don't.** Three reasons, none of them luck. The seller was verified before the buyer ever heard about the shares. The seller was a current employee who had not taken part in a company tender, thus keeping his transfer rights. Many others would have been blocked, and distinguishing between the two is a core part of a broker's job. And there was exactly one intermediary on each side. The number-one deal-killer I see is intermediary chains: co-brokers, inter-brokers, finders, informal advisors. Every additional link raises the odds of miscommunication — and of failure — exponentially.

One more cost note while we're being concrete: some companies charge a transfer fee, typically $5,000–$7,000. Be aware and discuss in advance who will pay. This is one reason minimum ticket sizes in directs are real — the fixed costs make small transactions impractical.

## Part 2: The Many Flavors of SPVs — and Which to Avoid

When a company blocks direct transfers — and more of the most sought-after names do every year — the SPV (Special Purpose Vehicle, or "fund") is usually the only door in. An SPV holds the shares; you buy an interest in the SPV. Simple in principle. In practice, the more sought after the underlying security, the more exotic the SPV can be, and the more layers.

**The clean SPV.** A single-layer vehicle — the fund itself owns the shares — with no management fee and no carry. In the market's shorthand: 0/0. These exist and I've placed clients into them. Since the manager is not making any money on the SPV itself, these are generally priced a bit above the company’s most recent round or current market.

**The standard SPV.** A fee structure written as two numbers: management fee / carry. 1/10 means 1% per year in management fees and 10% of the profits at exit. 2/20 is the traditional private-fund structure. Whether these are acceptable depends on what you're getting: real allocation access in a name you cannot otherwise touch may be worth 2/20. A riskier or less well known name may sell for 1/10. It all comes down to the market: if there are $10M of buyers and the seller has $5M of supply, the trade fills with those willing to pay 2/20 first. If there is only $1M of demand, fees may have to come down to 0/5.

**The layered SPV.** Some SPVs don't own shares — they own an interest in *another* SPV, which owns the shares (a double-layer). Layering isn't automatically abusive; it sometimes exists for legitimate structural reasons. But every layer is an opportunity for someone to charge fees, and a structure with management fees and carry stacked at each level can quietly consume a shocking share of your return before you see a dollar. Because of this, single-layer SPVs are much preferred — but the reality is that for some of the most sought-after names, only two-layer structures exist. Regardless, conduct your due diligence on both managers, L1 and L2.

**The syndication.** This occurs when a manager has access to an upcoming or ongoing round and raises capital into an SPV, which they will then use to make the purchase. This is how much of the market’s access to the most competitive rounds actually works, and it carries a real advantage: you pay the same price as every other investor in that round (net, before fees), rather than a marked-up secondary price — you are paying “market” on the underlying investment. However, since the manager is not making anything on the trade itself, just passing it along essentially at cost, they usually pay their bills by charging management fee and carry, or a one-time fee. Sometimes, if you see a 2/20 trade, you can offer a 15 or 20% one-time fee and the structure can be 0/0. Another trade-off is execution risk: the allocation can shrink or fall through, so work with syndicators who have a track record of actually securing allocations. And understand that the deadlines here are real — the round closes when the round closes.

**Before you wire into any SPV, you should be able to answer:** Does this vehicle own the shares today — and will the manager show evidence of the position? How many layers sit between me and the stock? What are the fees *at every layer*, including any exit or transfer fees? If this is not tied to a round or tender, at what price did the vehicle acquire the shares, and what am I paying per underlying share? Who is the manager, what's their track record, who does their fund administration, and how do they handle investor communications? If any of these questions changes the mood of the conversation, that may be the answer.

## Part 3: Direct Shares vs. SPVs — and How Buyers Actually Get Directs

**What "direct" means.** You own the stock. Your name (or your entity's) goes on the cap table, you're bound by the bylaws attached to those shares, and at an exit you're treated as the shareholder you are. No manager between you and your asset, no ongoing fees, no carry, no reliance on someone else's vehicle surviving until the IPO.

**What it costs you.** Time — the ROFR clock and settlement mean \~50 days versus a week for an SPV interest. Ticket size — transfer costs and company minimums make small directs impractical. Availability — the biggest constraint of all, because many of the most in-demand companies simply forbid direct transfers, historically including names like Anthropic and OpenAI. For those, the SPV isn't a choice; it's the only door.

**A word on share class.** Directs from employees are almost always **common** shares; the headline price you read about from a funding round is for **preferred**. Preferred carries real economic advantages — most importantly a liquidation preference: if the company is sold or wound down, preferred holders stand ahead of you in line. That's why common trades at a discount to preferred, and in soft markets I've seen that discount become very wide. For buyers with conviction in a company's future who can tolerate this "preference risk," accumulating common at a discount can meaningfully lower average cost basis. It is a risk decision, not a bargain by default — in a disappointing exit, the preference stack is not a technicality.

**How experienced buyers actually get directs.** Not by outbidding everyone — by being closeable. Work with a broker who has completed transactions *in that specific company*: every company has its own bylaws, process, and temperament around transfers, and a broker who has been through that company's process knows where the trapdoors are. Care about which seller you're matched with, because companies that selectively approve transfers judge the seller as much as the trade. Be positioned before the crowd arrives: when an IPO is announced or credibly rumored, prices spike, sellers pull back or demand premiums, and companies lock down the cap table — historically, the window where directs made sense was 18–24 months before a plausible exit, not the weeks before one. And be the counterparty who signs promptly, wires when agreed, and doesn't renegotiate after introduction. This market is small. Brokers bring the rare clean allocation to the buyers who closed cleanly last time.

## Part 4: What to Know Before Trading Through a Platform

Online platforms have brought real transparency to a market that had little. But "I'll just use a platform" is not the free lunch it appears to be, and you should walk in knowing four things.

**The fees are real even when they're quiet.** Platforms charge on the trade — reasonable, so does every broker. But many platform transactions are SPV-based, and SPV-based platforms often charge on the *exit* as well: a carry or fee when the position finally converts or distributes, years after you stopped thinking about the terms. A traditional brokered trade prices the commission into the transaction once; what you pay is what you paid. Even worse, some platforms don’t allow you to sell at all. Read the platform's fee schedule to the last line, especially the lines about what happens at distribution.

**The posted price is not your price.** A mark on a screen is an indication — an ask someone posted, an interpolation from stale trades, a model. It is not an executable quote. The spread between the number you saw and the number a real counterparty will transact at is where newcomers get their education. Treat marks as a sanity check on any price you're quoted — if a deal is far above or below the visible market, the likeliest outcome isn't that you've found an inefficiency; it's that the trade won't close.

**Your order travels farther than you think.** The universe of serious buyers and sellers in any name is small. Post your bid or your shares across multiple platforms and brokers, and copies of it converge on the same inboxes — creating the illusion of more demand or supply than exists, and moving the perceived price *against* you. Working a few channels at a time isn’t loyalty; it’s not bidding against yourself.

**A listing is not supply.** The same phantom-inventory problem from Part 1 lives on platforms too (although many are working to weed out these fake indications): postings for shares the poster doesn't control, at prices designed to harvest your contact information. The verification questions in Part 5 apply to a platform counterparty exactly as they do to a broker on the phone.

None of this is a case against platforms. It's a case for using them the way professionals do: as a data source and one possible venue — priced, like everything in this market, only when you know the full fee path from entry to exit.

## Part 5: Red Flags — and the Questions That Expose Them

After ten years, I can usually tell in one conversation whether a counterparty is real. Here's the pattern-matching, made explicit.

**Red flags in a trade:**

**Phantom inventory.** The most common failure in this market: shares that are talked about but never materialize. The offer is real; the shares aren't. The tell is specificity — real supply comes with a share class, an approximate size, a seller category, and a willingness to verify ownership through proper channels. Vague supply is no supply.

**"Allocation" without substance.** "We have allocation" can mean a signed agreement for a specific dollar amount in a specific round — or it can mean "we know someone who says they can get us in."

**Haste.** When you're being rushed, ask whose deadline you're serving. An unfunded SPV manager racing to raise, a broker chasing quarter-end — their urgency is not your urgency. (After a trade is matched and the transfer process is running, prompt responses genuinely matter. Before commitment, pressure is a flag.)

**Fortune-telling.** A broker's job is to facilitate transactions, not predict markets. When the pitch leans on where the price is going rather than what the trade *is* — structure, class, fees, process — you're hearing a story, not an offer. Ask for data: firm bids, asks, recent crossed trades.

**No compliance.** If nobody has asked you to verify who you are, be worried, not relieved. KYC/AML isn't friction; it's evidence you're dealing with a regulated counterparty who has something to lose. The absence of process is the presence of risk.

**A price too good.** Meaningfully below market and the ROFR takes it. Meaningfully above and it won't close. Exceptional deals exist in this market — I’ve seen a few, but mostly only in retrospect.

**Shape-shifting counterparties.** The "buyer" who turns out to be an intermediary for the real buyer. The "seller" collecting your cash to go try to buy shares they don't hold. The counterparty who agrees on terms, takes the introduction, then renegotiates directly. All three are known species. All three end relationships permanently — this market shares notes on bad actors.

**Questions to put to any broker:** Have you personally closed transactions in this specific company — and how does its transfer process work? Is this direct or an SPV — and if an SPV, will you show me the structure, every fee at every layer, and evidence of the underlying position? Who is the seller (by category, not name), how was ownership verified, and do they have the clear legal right to sell? Is your counterparty engaged and firm — proof of funds on the buy side, verified shares on the sell side — or is this an idea in search of parties? How do you manage the twenty-plus steps of this process — walk me through your roadmap, who's responsible for what, and what happens next.

**Questions a seller should be able to answer:** Are the shares fully vested and free of restrictions, liens, and tax complications? Is spousal consent required, and available? Have you completed KYC and provided ownership verification? Are you engaged with one broker on this, or is this offer traveling through several hands?

A counterparty who answers these readily is a counterparty you can transact with.

## Part 6: What a Decade of Closing Teaches You

Everything above is the market as it's described. This is the market as it behaves.

**For buyers**

**The real ROFR window.** Bylaws say 30 days. The company has no incentive to meet it, and most don't. In practice the window closes when the board next meets and someone on the legal side has been reminded more than once. Plan for the bylaws' number plus a board cycle, and work with a broker who is willing to do the reminding.

**Real sellers versus aspirational ones.** In any in-demand name, a large share of the "supply" is people who hope to get an allocation and are marketing it before they have it. They cost you weeks. I verify two things before a buyer hears about shares: evidence of the position or the allocation — correspondence with the company, ledger confirmation, or the equivalent — and the seller's track record. A seller who has closed before is a seller. One who hasn't is a maybe.

**When a two-layer SPV beats a single layer.** Layers aren't the enemy; stacked fees are. A two-layer structure where the all-in fees are lower than the single-layer alternative, and where both managers are established, active participants in this market, is the better vehicle. Compare the number that reaches you at exit, not the count of entities on the diagram.

**For sellers**

**Dataroom tourists.** Some buyers have no intention of buying. They want your financials to mark their own position, or to price an SPV they're thinking of launching. The pattern: an institutional fund that already holds the security and expects not to pay a fee. I rate every buyer on likelihood to close, using their transaction history, and only introduce the ones who score highly. Your dataroom should open after that screen, not before.

**The cost of a tender.** Company tenders feel like the safe liquidity path, and they often are. But in many companies, participating in a tender means giving up the right to transfer directly afterward. If you sell a portion now and expect to sell more later on your own terms, read what you're signing.

**For both sides**

**Three reliable ways to never close a trade.** First, negotiate the commission down. Pay market and focus on your net (seller) or gross (buyer) price. Even if you win, you've put yourself at the bottom of the list for any allocation that's in demand, and you've made it harder for your broker to source internally — at a firm with more than fifty brokers, fees are typically split with the counterparty's broker, and few will work a discounted trade. Second, resist compliance paperwork. Every delay raises a flag with everyone who will ever have to approve your trade. Third, break your word — renegotiate after introduction, or fall down on a signed trade. You will not transact with that brokerage again, and this market shares notes.

## The Part Nobody Puts in a Guide

Everything above is mechanics. What actually determines your long-term results in this market is simpler and less comfortable: this is a small, close-knit community — brokers, buyers, sellers, company CFOs, law firms — and it has a long memory. The people who misrepresent, renegotiate after introductions, commit and vanish, or treat counterparties badly find, gradually and then suddenly, that nobody brings them anything. The people who are straightforward about their goals, honor their written word, and stay courteous under stress get the first look when something rare opens up. I have watched both trajectories play out for a decade. Your reputation is the only position you hold in every trade.

And the permanent rule, which no structure or diligence changes: never invest more in this market than you could comfortably lose. Private securities can go to zero. The preference stack can leave common holders with nothing. Liquidity can fail to arrive for years past any plan. Treat every dollar you place here as a dollar you can wait on indefinitely — or lose entirely — and you'll make better decisions with all of them.
